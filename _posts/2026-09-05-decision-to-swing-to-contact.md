## The Question

A “quick swing” is one of the most familiar descriptions of a good baseball swing. It is also one of the least precisely measured.

Sometimes the phrase refers to a short-looking hand path. Sometimes it means high bat speed. Sometimes it describes a hitter who appears to wait longer before launching. More recently, Statcast has provided a direct measure of swing length: the total distance traveled by the barrel in three-dimensional space over the tracked portion of the swing.

Those qualities are related, but they are not interchangeable.

A barrel can travel a short distance slowly. It can travel a longer distance quickly. Two hitters can produce the same bat speed at contact after taking different amounts of time to get there. A hitter can also begin moving the pelvis early, delay the torso and hands, and still deliver the barrel rapidly during the final portion of the swing.

This creates a question that ordinary bat-speed testing does not answer.

The traditional kinetic-chain model emphasizes a proximal-to-distal sequence. The pelvis reaches peak rotational velocity first, followed by the torso, arm, hand, and ultimately the bat. This progression is intended to transfer and amplify speed as energy moves toward the barrel.

Ronald Acuña Jr. provides a useful visual reference for this high-output idea: an explosive lower-body turn appears to feed an increasingly fast barrel. That comparison is illustrative, not a claim that publicly available research has measured Acuña’s complete segment-onset and peak-velocity sequence.

But maximizing the speed of the endpoint may not minimize the total time required to move it.

What if a hitter rotates the pelvis, torso, lead arm, hands, and bat more nearly together? That movement may not create the same delayed acceleration or peak bat speed. Yet if it also produces a shorter barrel path and less delay between segments, the bat could conceivably arrive sooner.

Luis Arraez provides the contrasting visual reference. His swing often appears shorter and more direct, and MLB has published an individual Arraez swing with a Statcast swing length of 4.4 feet. That measurement supports the short-barrel-path description; it does not establish that his body segments initiate simultaneously.

That possibility matters because a shorter movement could allow the hitter to begin later and gather more information before launch. Hitters may continue processing and adjusting after movement begins, so this is a potential advantage rather than an automatic one.

> **Working question**  
> Can a shorter barrel path combined with more simultaneous segment movement reduce the total time from the first swing-producing movement to contact, even if the swing produces less bat speed than a more pronounced proximal-to-distal sequence?

This is not simply a question of contact hitting versus power hitting. It is a question of what each movement strategy buys and what it costs.

## What Current Research Says

### Where does a swing actually begin?

Before swing time can be measured, the beginning of the swing must be defined.

That sounds straightforward. It is not.

A hitter begins organizing movement before the barrel visibly accelerates toward the ball. The front foot may leave the ground. Pressure may shift toward the rear leg and then forward. The pelvis may begin opening while the torso remains closed. The hands may load backward before changing direction. The handle may start moving while the barrel still appears to stay behind the hitter.

Each of those events can serve as a starting point, but each answers a different question.

- Pelvis onset captures an early part of the rotational sequence, but it may include preparatory movement that occurs before the hitter is committed to swinging.
- Torso onset captures a later part of the chain while excluding lower-body movement already underway.
- Hand onset provides a recognizable measure of bat delivery, but the lower body may have been rotating for a substantial period before it occurs.
- Knob or handle onset gives an implement-based landmark. A knob-mounted sensor such as Blast Motion uses a proprietary “Transition Event” to calculate Time to Contact from the start of the downswing to impact. That is conceptually similar, although it does not represent the beginning of the whole-body action.
- Barrel onset isolates the final delivery period but omits nearly everything that created it.
- Electromyography (EMG) could detect when relevant muscles become active, but preparatory muscle activity can also occur on pitches the hitter eventually takes.
- Electroencephalography (EEG) could identify neural activity associated with perception, response selection, and motor preparation, but it would provide a model-dependent decision proxy rather than one exact moment of conscious choice. Full-speed swinging would also introduce substantial movement and muscle artifact.

Researchers have made different choices. In a study of 17 elite softball hitters facing randomly mixed fast and slow pitches, swing onset was defined from the velocity of one hand relative to the pelvis. The threshold was set at 10 percent of each hitter’s mean peak hand velocity to avoid classifying early fluctuations as the swing. A 2025 virtual-reality study of 23 professional players instead defined initiation as the point at which bat speed exceeded 4 meters per second, with additional criteria used to confirm that a swing followed.

These are defensible operational definitions. They are not the same event.

Therefore, two studies can report different swing durations without their hitters necessarily moving differently. The clock itself may have started at a different point.

### A composite start would measure the whole sequence

For the present question, the most useful primary clock would begin with the first qualifying swing-producing movement across several tracked segments.

The pelvis, torso, lead arm, hands, handle, and barrel would each be measured. The clock would start when the first of those segments makes a sustained movement in the forward swing direction after completing its loading or counter-movement phase. Bat–ball contact would stop the clock.

This can be called **composite swing time**.

The word *qualifying* is important. A bat waggle, a small balance correction, or backward movement during the load should not start the clock. The onset-detection method would need to be chosen before analyzing the results, justified through prior research or reliability testing, and tested under alternative definitions. There is no established universal velocity threshold that automatically separates preparation from the swing for every body segment.

Composite swing time would not reveal when the hitter consciously decided to swing. Some early movement may occur while the hitter is still gathering information and preserving the option to stop. Instead, the measure would answer a narrower question: how much time elapsed between the first observable movement contributing to the forward swing and contact?

The individual timestamps would remain valuable. The interval between the first and last segment onsets would describe **initiation spread**. A smaller spread would represent a more simultaneous initiation pattern. A larger spread would represent a more extended progression from the earliest segment to the latest.

Peak-velocity order would be measured separately. Beginning to move and reaching maximum speed are different mechanical events.

### What does Statcast swing length measure?

Statcast defines swing length as the cumulative distance traveled by the head of the bat in three-dimensional space from the beginning of the available tracking data to the impact point. That tracking generally covers approximately the final 150 milliseconds before contact.

In other words, swing length is a distance—not an elapsed time.

The distinction is easy to miss because a shorter path often looks quicker. Statcast’s results from the first month of 2024 showed the expected population-level tradeoff: shorter-than-average swings made contact more often and whiffed less, while longer-than-average swings produced more slugging. But Statcast also notes that swing speed and swing length overlap imperfectly. Hitters can travel similar distances with meaningfully different speeds.

The public metric also does not identify the beginning of the whole-body swing. If the pelvis or torso starts moving before the tracking window, that movement is not added to swing length. A hitter could therefore have a short measured barrel path while using a relatively long preparatory sequence—or a long measured path that is traversed extremely quickly.

[MLB Statcast: Swing Length](https://www.mlb.com/glossary/statcast/swing-length){: .source-link }

### Shorter distance does not guarantee shorter time

At its simplest, movement time depends on both distance and average speed. A shorter path creates the possibility of a shorter movement, but only if the velocity profile does not offset that advantage.

A longer swing may include more time and distance over which to accelerate the barrel. A hitter who uses that distance well could cover a longer route in the same time—or even less time—than a hitter whose shorter swing accelerates slowly.

The location of contact also changes the calculation. In a study of 28 professional and university hitters facing tossed balls, bat-head speed tended to increase when contact occurred farther forward, lower, and more inside. Swing time also changed with contact location. A path is therefore partly a response to where the hitter must meet the ball rather than an isolated mechanical preference.

This is why the proposed hypothesis requires both measurements. Barrel-path length cannot stand in for composite swing time, and composite swing time cannot describe the shape of the route.

[Morishita et al., 2019](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jjpehss/64/2/64_18058/_article/-char/en){: .source-link }

### Hitters do not simply wait and then execute a fixed swing

Research on swing timing suggests that hitters organize several overlapping phases around the flight of the pitch.

Katsumata divided hitting into stepping, landing, weighting the front foot, initiating the swing, and impact. When hitters faced different pitch speeds, the timing relationships among these phases changed in a compensatory manner. Variability became progressively smaller as the movement approached contact.

That pattern suggests that timing is not controlled through one isolated start command. Earlier and later phases can compensate for one another.

Later work using a virtual batting environment reached a similar conclusion. Changes in one phase could be offset by changes in another while preserving the total time required to reach the hitting zone. This was described as functional—or “good”—variability. The exact movement solution also differed among hitters.

This matters for the compact-swing hypothesis. A hitter with a longer total sequence might still delay a more consequential part of the swing. Another hitter might initiate several segments together but make more adjustment during the remaining movement. One number will not fully describe either strategy.

[Katsumata, 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17204344/){: .source-link }

[Gray, 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.01295/full){: .source-link }

### Movement onset appears to contain useful perceptual information

The Nasu softball study provides especially relevant evidence.

The hitters faced fastballs and slowballs in random order. Hitters who produced a larger difference between their swing-onset timing for the two speeds also produced better experimental outcomes: higher exit velocity and fewer misses. Those batting-task outcomes explained more of the variation in real-game batting average than a separate button-press judgment task.

This does not show that one absolute swing duration is best. It suggests that the ability to adapt the timing of movement to the expected arrival of the ball is part of batting skill.

The 2025 virtual-reality study also found that professional hitters adjusted their initiation timing as pitch flight time changed. When the ball was visually removed 150 milliseconds after release, some timing behavior remained intact while other aspects of swing decision and adjustment were impaired. The broader lesson is that the perceptual, decision, and movement portions of batting overlap rather than occurring in a perfectly clean sequence.

A shorter composite swing could theoretically allow later initiation. However, that benefit must be demonstrated by measuring onset relative to pitch release. It cannot be assumed from a short barrel path alone.

### What does the kinetic sequence actually measure?

The classic description of the baseball swing follows a proximal-to-distal progression. Early three-dimensional work reported the hips reaching peak rotational speed before the shoulders, followed by high bat speed. Comparisons of hitters at different skill levels have also found that higher-level hitters tend to produce greater segment velocities and greater bat velocity.

These findings support the importance of coordinated whole-body rotation. They do not establish one universal amount of delay between segments, nor do they show that maximizing the delay minimizes the time required to reach contact.

Kinematic-sequence assessments commonly rank the times at which segments reach peak angular velocity. A textbook sequence might be pelvis, torso, arm, forearm, and hand. Yet the batting-specific evidence is less uniform than this model can sound.

In a 2020 conference study of 23 baseball players and 47 swings, researchers identified 11 sequence patterns. No batter displayed the exact prescribed proximal-to-distal sequence. Five trials in which the forearm and hand peaked simultaneously were grouped as the closest representation. That group had lower peak elbow-extension torque, suggesting a possible joint-loading benefit, but the study was not evidence that the textbook order maximized bat speed or batting performance.

A 2024 conference report involving 129 players found that the prescribed hip-to-torso-to-arm-to-hand peak order occurred in 38.8 percent of participants. After controlling for age, height, weight, and measured physical capacities, sequence classification did not predict average or peak exit velocity. Arm and hand rotational speeds were associated with exit velocity, but hip and torso speeds were not.

These studies should be interpreted cautiously. Both were conference reports, the samples included mixed ages and competition levels, and neither directly tested the present time-to-contact hypothesis. They do show why a coaching ideal should not be treated as a settled biological law.

### Sequence onset and peak sequence are not the same question

This distinction is central to the proposed study.

One hitter might begin rotating the pelvis 200 milliseconds before contact, begin moving the hands 130 milliseconds before contact, and reach peak velocities in a clear proximal-to-distal order. Another might begin the pelvis, torso, and hands within a narrow window but still reach their peaks at different times.

The second hitter has more simultaneous **onsets** without necessarily having simultaneous **peaks**.

If the purpose of a kinetic-sequence assessment is to understand how speed is transferred into the barrel, peak order may be informative. If the purpose is to determine how long the complete movement takes, onset timing is indispensable.

Existing batting studies more often describe peak angular velocities, positions at mechanical events, or bat speed at contact than the complete set of segment-onset times. Research has examined swing phases and hand or bat onset, but little work has synchronized those measurements with three-dimensional barrel distance and the full sequence of individual body-segment onsets.

Consequently, the main hypothesis remains largely unanswered.

### A shorter swing could also reduce the timing window

Reaching one contact point quickly is not the only temporal requirement in hitting. The bat and ball must occupy compatible locations at compatible times.

A 2025 study combined 18 pitched-ball trajectories with 145 dry-swing trajectories from collegiate players. The average calculated range in which the bat’s sweet spot could contact the ball was 9.36 milliseconds. Yet individual combinations ranged from 2.48 to 30.40 milliseconds.

The difference depended on the three-dimensional relationship among the pitch trajectory, the direction of the bat’s sweet-spot path, and the angle of the bat. Aligning the bat and pitch in a side view was helpful but insufficient.

This creates an important counterargument to the shortest-path hypothesis. A hitter could take the geometrically shortest route to one predicted contact point while creating a very small window for error. A somewhat longer route that places the barrel on a compatible three-dimensional path could be more forgiving.

The study did not observe real pitcher-batter matchups; pitch and swing trajectories were recorded separately and superimposed. It nevertheless demonstrates that quick arrival and temporal coverage are separate properties.

[Nakashima et al., 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2025.1557145/full){: .source-link }

### The relevant optimum depends on the hitter’s goal

There may be no single ideal position on the contact–power spectrum.

A contact-oriented hitter may benefit from a shorter path and shorter composite time even if the movement sacrifices some bat speed. A power-oriented hitter may accept earlier initiation or a longer sequence in exchange for greater collision speed. The same hitter may move along this spectrum with two strikes, when anticipating a particular pitch, or when trying to maximize damage in a favorable count.

Statcast’s early swing-length results are consistent with this general tradeoff at the population level: shorter swings made more contact but produced less power, while longer swings missed more frequently but generated more slugging. Those descriptive results do not identify the cause. Pitch selection, count, location, hitter intent, and contact point can all affect the measured swing.

The useful question is therefore not which end of the spectrum is universally correct. It is whether the movement pattern matches the hitter’s goal without creating unnecessary time, path, or contact-quality costs.

### What we know and what we do not

Current evidence supports several conclusions:

- Swing length and swing time are different measurements.
- Hitters adjust movement timing to pitch speed and can compensate across phases of the swing.
- Higher segment and bat velocities are associated with higher-level hitting and greater batted-ball speed.
- Three-dimensional bat-path alignment can meaningfully change the timing window for contact.
- The exact textbook peak-velocity sequence is not universal in the available batting studies.

Publicly available research has not established:

- Whether shorter Statcast-style barrel paths produce shorter composite swing times within the same hitter
- Whether smaller delays among segment onsets reduce total time to contact
- Whether a more simultaneous onset pattern necessarily reduces bat speed
- Whether any time saved permits later initiation against real pitches
- Whether later initiation improves swing decisions, contact, or both
- Where the best time–speed tradeoff lies for hitters with different offensive goals

Major-league organizations and private training facilities may already examine some of these relationships internally. Without published methods and results, however, those findings cannot be independently evaluated or treated as established evidence.

Answering those questions requires measuring the complete movement instead of treating bat speed, barrel path, or kinematic sequence as a substitute for time.

## A Better Study

The proposed study would collect many representative swings from each hitter, allowing within-hitter comparisons whenever reliably distinguishable movement patterns emerge. The goal would not be to label one body pattern correct. It would be to determine which measurable combinations of path, sequence, and time produce which outcomes.

### Participants

The study would recruit approximately 30 to 40 advanced collegiate, minor-league, or comparably skilled hitters.

Each hitter would face enough pitches to estimate his individual relationships among path length, initiation timing, pitch difficulty, and outcome. A target of approximately 150 analyzable swings per hitter would provide far more within-hitter information than the five or ten swings common in small laboratory studies.

### Pitch and intent conditions

Hitters would face randomized pitch speeds, types, movements, and locations from a programmable machine or live pitchers. They would not know the upcoming pitch.

The primary session would use the hitter’s natural game swing. This would preserve representative coordination and allow swings to be analyzed as continuous measurements rather than forcing athletes into artificial categories.

A secondary session could vary intent:

- Contact-preservation or two-strike intent
- Normal game intent
- Maximum-damage intent

The instructions would not define the mechanics. Motion data would determine whether a hitter actually shortened the barrel path, compressed segment-onset timing, or changed bat speed. This would also test whether the preferred tradeoff moves with the hitter’s goal.

### Synchronized measurements

The study would synchronize:

- High-speed three-dimensional motion capture of the pelvis, torso, lead arm, hands, handle, and barrel
- Force plates under both feet
- Pitch tracking
- High-speed video of bat–ball contact
- Bat and batted-ball tracking

Surface electromyography could be added to compare muscle activation with kinematic onset. EEG could be explored in a separate controlled task, but it would not define the primary clock.

All signals would be aligned to pitch release, mechanical loading reversals, each segment’s forward onset, each segment’s peak velocity, entry into the hitting region, and bat–ball contact or closest approach.

### Defining the start

For every tracked segment, researchers would identify the first sustained movement in the swing-producing direction after that segment’s final loading reversal. The earliest qualifying timestamp across all tracked segments would define composite onset.

The onset method would be preregistered. Its parameters would be supported by relevant research or pilot reliability testing rather than presented as universal values. A change-point method and several reasonable velocity thresholds would be compared to determine whether the findings depend on how onset was defined.

Swings in which qualifying movement begins before pitch release would be retained and identified as pre-release initiations. They should not be forced to begin at release, because doing so would artificially shorten the recorded movement.

### Path and sequence variables

Compactness would be represented by the cumulative three-dimensional distance traveled by the barrel.

The study would calculate:

- Full barrel travel from the barrel’s forward onset to contact
- Statcast-compatible barrel travel over the final approximately 150 milliseconds
- Composite onset-to-contact time
- Initiation spread from the first segment onset to the last
- Pelvis-to-torso, torso-to-hand, and hand-to-barrel onset delays
- Peak-velocity order and the temporal gaps between peaks
- Average and peak barrel speed
- Time to peak barrel speed
- Three-dimensional pitch–bat alignment
- Acceptable timing-window width

This would reveal whether a shorter swing is genuinely quicker, whether it is merely measured over a shorter route, and whether concurrency contributes independently of barrel distance.

### Performance outcomes

The primary outcomes would be:

- Contact versus miss
- Closest bat–ball distance on misses
- Early or late timing error
- Squared-up percentage on contact
- Bat speed at contact
- Exit velocity

Pitch–bat intersection time would be included so that a path that reaches one point quickly is not automatically judged superior to a path that creates a wider opportunity for contact.

### Analysis

The analysis would first determine whether an individual hitter demonstrates reliably distinguishable movement patterns across repeated swings. If he does, the primary analysis would compare that hitter with himself across those patterns. If he does not, barrel length, onset spread, and the other mechanical variables would remain continuous rather than forcing his swings into artificial groups.

First, it would test whether shorter barrel paths predict shorter composite swing times. Second, it would test whether smaller initiation spreads predict additional reductions in time after accounting for barrel length. Third, it would examine whether shorter times and smaller spreads are associated with lower, equal, or higher bat speed.

The performance models would then test whether later pitch-relative onset, shorter composite time, and different path–sequence combinations predict contact, miss distance, timing accuracy, squared-up contact, and exit velocity.

Models would account for pitch velocity, type, movement, location, contact point, hitter intent, and the hitter’s own typical mechanics. Relationships would be examined continuously rather than dividing every swing into simplistic “good sequence” and “bad sequence” categories. The constraints-led intent conditions would describe the situation—protect with two strikes, take a normal game swing, or hunt damage—without instructing the hitter to shorten his path, rotate simultaneously, or alter his mechanics. Any movement changes would have to emerge from the hitter’s own solution.

## My Hypothesis

### H1: Shorter barrel paths will usually reduce composite swing time

I hypothesize that, within the same hitter, swings with shorter three-dimensional barrel paths will generally require less time from composite onset to contact.

This relationship will not be perfect. A longer path can be traversed quickly through greater acceleration, while a short path can still be slow. Nevertheless, reducing the distance the barrel must travel should create an opportunity to reduce total movement time.

### H2: More simultaneous initiation will reduce time beyond path length alone

I hypothesize that swings with smaller delays among the pelvis, torso, lead arm, hands, handle, and barrel will be completed more quickly, even after accounting for barrel-path distance.

This does not require all segments to reach peak velocity at the same time. The prediction concerns when they begin contributing to the forward swing. A hitter could initiate several segments within a narrow window and still produce a proximal-to-distal order of peak speeds.

### H3: A larger sequencing delay may create bat speed at a time cost

I hypothesize that some hitters will produce greater bat speed when their segment peaks are separated in a pronounced proximal-to-distal pattern. The possible cost is that the complete movement begins earlier or takes longer to finish.

This prediction is less certain than it may sound. The limited batting-specific evidence does not show that the textbook sequence always produces greater exit velocity, and different hitters may generate speed through different coordination patterns.

### H4: The practical value will depend on offensive intent

For a contact-oriented hitter, the benefit of a shorter composite time may outweigh a modest loss of bat speed. For a power-oriented hitter, the added value of greater bat speed may justify a longer or earlier-starting movement.

The same tradeoff may change within one plate appearance. With two strikes, the hitter may prefer greater time and contact coverage. In a favorable count with a strong pitch expectation, the hitter may accept earlier commitment to maximize damage.

I therefore do not expect one universal ideal sequence. I expect the best combination of path, onset timing, and bat speed to depend on the hitter’s abilities and goal.

### H5: The shortest route will not always be the most hittable route

Finally, I hypothesize that an extremely short path can become counterproductive if it reduces three-dimensional alignment with the pitch.

The most effective compact swing should not merely reach a single predicted point quickly. It should preserve enough pitch–bat intersection time to tolerate ordinary errors in prediction and timing.

> **Predicted pattern**  
> Shorter barrel travel and a compressed spread of segment onsets should reduce total swing time. More pronounced sequencing may add bat speed, while better three-dimensional path alignment may add timing tolerance. The preferred balance should move with the hitter’s contact–power goal.

## Conclusion

A “quick swing” should describe a measured interval, not an impression.

Statcast swing length has made barrel distance visible, but distance is not time. Bat-speed testing measures how fast the barrel is moving near contact, but it does not reveal how long the hitter took to produce that speed. Kinematic-sequence testing describes the order of segment peaks, but it does not automatically identify the beginning or duration of the swing.

These measurements answer different questions.

The proposed hypothesis is that a shorter barrel path combined with more simultaneous segment initiation may reduce the time from the first swing-producing movement to contact. That pattern could allow later initiation and more pitch information, even if it does not maximize bat speed in a controlled test.

The opposite tradeoff may also be valuable. A hitter may accept a longer or earlier-starting sequence because the resulting bat speed produces more damage. Another may prefer a shorter movement because contact is his primary constraint. The same hitter may need both solutions in different counts.

Current research does not tell us where those tradeoffs lie. It rarely measures complete segment onsets, barrel-path distance, total movement time, pitch-relative initiation, and contact outcomes in the same swing.

A useful assessment would do exactly that. It would start the clock from a transparent composite kinematic event, retain every individual timestamp, measure the barrel’s three-dimensional route, and evaluate the result against both contact and power.

Until those measurements are connected, neither “shorter is quicker” nor “better sequencing is more efficient” should be treated as a complete explanation.

> The relevant question is not only how fast the bat is moving at contact. It is how much movement—and how much time—the hitter needed to get it there.
